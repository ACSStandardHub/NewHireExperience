  • Solves and anticipates the next need

Core Values

The H.O.M.E Framework

Everything we do is anchored in four values. These are not slogans. They are the lens through which every decision, every interaction, and every moment on shift should be filtered.

H

Hospitality

Every resident deserves warmth, dignity, and attentiveness — regardless of how they're presenting in the moment. Hospitality is not contingent on how the other person behaves.

O

Ownership

If you see it, it's yours. Don't walk past a problem hoping someone else handles it. Take it, solve it, and document it. Your name is on this shift.

M

You are always being observed — by residents, management, cameras, and peers. Mindfulness means awareness: of your environment, your energy, and the impression you leave.

E

Excellence

Not perfection — but the relentless pursuit of it. Excellence means you never accept "good enough" when better is possible. In small things and large ones.

First Impressions

The 7-Second Standard

Research is consistent: people form a lasting impression within 7 seconds of meeting someone. In luxury residential environments, this matters more than almost anywhere else. The residents you serve chose to live in a premium environment — and you are part of what makes it feel that way.

⬡ Core Principle

Your uniform is not clothing — it is a signal. It communicates that this building has standards, and that those standards are upheld by every person who represents it.

Appearance Standards

Non-Negotiables

⬡ Uniform & Grooming
  • Uniform clean, pressed, and worn correctly at all times — no exceptions
  • Name badge visible and properly positioned on the left chest
  • Shoes polished or clean, appropriate to the property
  • Grooming: clean, neat, and professional. Hair secured if applicable
  • No visible torn, stained, or wrinkled clothing — ever
  • Minimal fragrance — luxury environments are scent-sensitive
  • No excessive jewelry or accessories that conflict with the professional image
⬡ Desk & Lobby Presence
  • Posture: upright, engaged, and approachable — not slouched or leaning
  • Eye contact initiated — not waited for
  • No personal phone at the desk during active shifts
  • Eating at the desk: never during peak hours, never visible to residents
  • Earbuds or headphones: prohibited during active coverage
  • Personal conversations: ended the moment a resident enters your sightline
Energy & Tone

The Energy You Carry is the Energy You Create

Lobby energy is contagious. Residents who walk into a space where the concierge is disengaged, tired, or visibly checked-out feel it immediately — and it colors their entire experience of the building. Agents who are composed, warm, and engaged create an atmosphere of calm professionalism that residents notice and trust.

❌ Drains the Lobby
  • Heavy sighing or visible fatigue
  • Monotone or flat vocal tone
  • Reactive — waits to be addressed
  • Distracted or unfocused
  • Complaining or venting near residents
✦ Elevates the Lobby
  • Composed and calm even during stress
  • Warm, clear vocal tone — measured and confident
  • Proactive — acknowledges before asked
  • Fully present, no matter the hour
  • Keeps internal issues internal
Voice & Communication

How You Say It Matters as Much as What You Say

  • Greet with Intention

    "Good morning / afternoon / evening, [name]" — Always use the time of day. Always use their name when you know it. These are not formalities. They are recognitions of personhood.

  • No Slang, No Shortcuts

    "Yeah," "Nope," "Sure," "No worries" — These are off-brand. Use: "Of course," "Certainly," "I'd be happy to," "Absolutely." These are not pretentious. They are precise.

  • De-escalate Through Tone, Not Just Words

    When a resident is upset, your voice is your most powerful tool. Slow down. Lower your pitch slightly. Don't match their energy — anchor it.

  • Never Say "I Can't"

    Replace with: "Let me find someone who can help with that" or "What I can do is..." — Solutions-forward language, always.

Shift Logs

Why Documentation Is Not Optional

Your shift log is not a formality. It is a legal record, a communication tool, and a professional calling card — all in one. What you document (or fail to document) affects property management decisions, incident response, and your own credibility as an agent.

Multiple audiences read your logs: the next shift agent, your supervisor, the property manager, and in some cases, building ownership or legal teams. Write accordingly.

⬡ Non-Negotiable

If it happened on your shift and it isn't documented, it did not happen. Period. Undocumented events are a liability — for you and for the property.

The Framework

E.D.O — Event, Detail, Outcome

Every log entry follows this method. It is simple, consistent, and covers everything that matters.

E
Event
What happened? State the incident, interaction, or observation clearly and factually. No opinions, no speculation.
D
Detail
Who was involved? What time? What location? What was said or done? The details are what make the log useful and defensible.
O
Outcome
How did it resolve? What action did you take? What still requires follow-up? Every entry needs a resolution or a next step.
⬡ E.D.O in Practice — Examples
  • Weak Log: "Resident was upset about a package." → Incomplete. No name, no time, no action taken, no outcome.
  • Apex Log: "8:47 PM — Resident in Unit 804 (Mr. Torres) approached desk stating package marked 'delivered' in Yardi was not found in Package Room. Package Room searched — item not located. Management notified via text at 8:52 PM. Carrier contact number provided to resident. Follow-up required morning shift."
Practice: Write an E.D.O log entry for this situation — "A smoke detector alarm triggered on the 8th floor at 11:15 PM. You investigated and found a resident cooking with the door open. The alarm stopped after 3 minutes."
★ Use specific language. Write as if you won't be there to explain it.
Desk Standards

The Desk Is Never Abandoned

Your desk is the control center of the building's front-of-house operation. When it is unmanned, the building has no face — and no one accountable for whatever walks through the door.

⬡ Desk Coverage Rules
  • The desk is never left unattended without coordinating relief coverage
  • If you need to leave briefly (restroom, emergency), notify management or a fellow agent
  • Desk area must be clean, organized, and professional at all times
  • All logs and communication must be up to date before any break
  • Handoff to incoming agent includes verbal and written summary — nothing left assumed
  • Never leave mid-incident. If something is active, you see it through before transition
Visibility on Site

Presence as a Service

Your physical presence throughout the building is part of the service. Concierge agents who stay planted behind a desk all shift create blind spots — in security, in resident relations, and in their own awareness of what the property needs.

  • Lobby Rounds

    Periodic walkthroughs of common areas: lobby, mail room, package room, elevators, and any accessible amenity spaces. Documented in the shift log with any observations.

  • Proactive Observation

    Look for what doesn't belong: lights out in common areas, spills, damaged property, strangers in secure zones, or anything that would concern a resident if they noticed it first.

  • Elevator Banks & Amenities

    During busy hours, visible presence near high-traffic areas adds an extra layer of both security and service. Your presence signals control.

Communication Expectations

Clear, Documented, and Timely

Management Updates
Any incident, complaint, or unusual event must be communicated to your supervisor before the shift ends — via text, email, or platform message. Do not assume they saw the log.
Resident Callbacks
If you commit to following up with a resident, it must be documented and tracked. "I'll let you know" with no action taken is worse than not promising anything.
Handoff Protocol
Verbal briefing to incoming agent. Key open items, pending tasks, active resident situations, and anything the next agent needs to know to start informed.

Decision Framework First

The A.C.T. Method

Before we run scenarios, you need a framework for when things go sideways. Under pressure, people default to instinct. Your job is to train your instinct to follow this:

A

Acknowledge

Recognize what's happening. Name it internally. Do not react — respond. "This resident is upset. My role is to de-escalate, not defend."

C

Control

Control the environment, then the conversation. Move to a quieter space if possible. Lower your voice. Create psychological space.

T

Transfer or Resolve

Either resolve it directly within your authority, or transfer to the correct escalation point — and document both the action and outcome.

Scenario Work

Think Through These

Scenario 01 The Noise Complaint
It's 11:30 PM on a Friday. Resident in 6B calls the desk furious — the unit above has been blasting music for two hours. She's demanded action three times already. She's done being polite.

What do you do? Walk through A.C.T.

Apex Approach

Acknowledge her frustration without dismissing it. Go to the floor — knock on 7B, introduce yourself professionally, and address the noise directly but without confrontation. Return to 6B with an update. Document the entire interaction: times, residents, actions, outcome. If the noise continues, escalate to management per protocol. Never promise outcomes you cannot control.

Scenario 02 Smoking in a Non-Smoking Building
You smell cigarette smoke near the 3rd floor stairwell. You find a male guest — not a resident — smoking. He's hostile immediately: "I'm a guest of a resident. You can't tell me what to do."

How do you handle this without escalating — and without backing down?

Apex Approach

Stay calm. You are not in a confrontation — you are enforcing a policy that protects all residents. "I understand you're a guest here. This is a non-smoking building — that policy applies to everyone on the property. I'll need to ask you to step outside." If he refuses, do not escalate physically. Retreat, contact management, and document. Your job is not to win — it's to address, record, and escalate appropriately.

Scenario 03 The Difficult Resident
Resident in unit 1202 has a reputation. She regularly makes demands that aren't in scope, speaks condescendingly to staff, and threatens to "call the management company" for any perceived slight. She approaches the desk today clearly agitated.

How do you engage — and protect yourself and the team — without losing your composure or creating a new problem?

Apex Approach

Don't alter your baseline because of a reputation. Every interaction starts fresh. Listen fully before responding. Speak clearly and neutrally. Validate her concern without validating poor behavior: "I hear your frustration. Here's what I'm able to do for you right now." If she crosses into abusive territory, you are permitted to calmly redirect: "I want to help you, and I'll be able to do that best if we can keep this conversation respectful." Document everything — tone, words, time, and outcome.

Scenario 04 Unauthorized Person in the Building
You notice someone in the lobby who has been sitting for 45 minutes. They did not check in, do not appear to be waiting for a resident, and become vague when asked who they're visiting.

What is your protocol? At what point do you escalate?

Apex Approach

Approach professionally and with genuine hospitality — not accusation. "Good afternoon. Can I help you find someone or let a resident know you're here?" If the answer is evasive, inform them the lobby is for residents and authorized guests, and offer to help connect them. If they refuse to leave or cannot be verified, contact management immediately and document the situation. Security is a service — not a confrontation. Treat it as such while still protecting the property.

⬡

Key Takeaway: In every scenario above, notice that the Apex agent never loses composure, never makes a promise they can't keep, and always documents. These are not coincidences — they are trained behaviors.

90-Day Expectations Attendance Policy Ownership Mindset
The 90-Day Window

This Period Defines Everything

Your first 90 days are not probation — they are your foundation. Every habit you build in this period will either serve you or cost you for the rest of your time with Apex. People don't re-define themselves easily after the first 90 days. The behaviors you establish now are the behaviors that follow you.

⬡ Leadership Observation

Leadership forms opinions fast. The agent who is consistently on time, engaged, and professional from Day 1 does not have to fight to be taken seriously. The agent who establishes a pattern of inconsistency does — and often it's too late before they realize it.

Attendance Policy

Your Presence Is Your First Responsibility

On-Time Standard
Scheduled start time means you are in position, in uniform, and ready to receive briefing — not walking in the door. Arrive 10 minutes early as the standard. 5 minutes early is the minimum.
Call-Out Protocol
Calling out requires direct contact with your supervisor — text or phone — no later than 2 hours before shift start. "Calling out" through a group chat or messaging a coworker does not count and is not accepted.
No-Call / No-Show
A no-call, no-show is a serious violation. First occurrence triggers a formal conversation. Second occurrence within 90 days may result in termination. There is no exception for "I forgot."
Shift Swaps
You may arrange a swap with a qualified colleague, but it must be submitted for management approval before the shift — not the night before. Uncleared swaps are not swaps. They are absences.
Coverage Responsibility
If you are the last on shift and no relief arrives, you do not leave. You contact management immediately. The building cannot go uncovered. Your exit from a shift must be a clean handoff — not a disappearance.
Ownership Mindset

You Are Not a Vendor. You Are a Stakeholder.

The agents who advance here — who become leads, who get placed at our most prestigious properties — are the ones who operate like they have a stake in the outcome. They don't wait to be told. They don't do the minimum. They treat every shift like their performance is being assessed — because it is.

Ownership means you take responsibility for your shift, your environment, and your impact — before anyone has to ask you to.

  • Own Your Shift

    Don't count the minutes until you leave. Invest in the hours you're there. Find what needs to be done and do it. The agents who are noticed are the ones who find the problem no one assigned them.

  • Own Your Mistakes

    If you make an error — and you will — own it immediately. Bring it to your supervisor with a proposed solution. Hiding mistakes makes them bigger. Addressing them builds trust.

  • Own Your Development

    Ask questions. Seek feedback. Study the property. Know the residents' names. Know the building systems. The agent who treats this as a learning environment grows fast. The one who coasts stays stagnant.

What Early Leaders Do

The Behaviors That Get Noticed

  • They Raise the Floor, Not Just Their Own Bar

    When a high performer operates at a standard, others around them often rise to meet it — even without being asked. Your consistency becomes a silent form of coaching. Never underestimate the influence of modeling excellence.

  • They Solve, Don't Complain

    Every shift has friction. Agents who spend their energy on grievances stagnate. Agents who redirect that energy toward solutions get trusted with more responsibility — and more opportunity.

  • They Know the Property Cold

    Building knowledge is a form of authority. The agent who knows every amenity space, every elevator bank, every vendor protocol, and every resident's name becomes indispensable — because they're the person everyone asks.

  • They Protect the Culture

    Apex agents do not tolerate shortcuts, gossip, or low standards from their peers — not loudly, not aggressively, but through the steady example of doing things the right way, every time.

  • They Ask For Feedback Before It's Offered

    The agents who grow fastest are the ones who actively seek to be corrected. This is not weakness — it is intelligence. It communicates to leadership: this person wants to get better. That person gets invested in.

Setting the Tone on Shift

Your Shift Starts Before It Starts

How you arrive sets the tone for the next 8 hours. The agent who comes in distracted, underprepared, or visibly uninterested poisons the well before the first resident interaction. The agent who arrives composed, informed, and ready sends the opposite signal — to their team, to residents, and to themselves.

  • Confirm your uniform is correct and your appearance is professional

  • Arrive with the posture you want to keep for the next 8 hours
